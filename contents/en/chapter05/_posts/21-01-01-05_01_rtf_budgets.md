**Real-time factor (RTF)** is wall-clock time divided by the duration of the audio you just processed. If a 10 ms hop takes 12 ms, the callback misses and the listener hears a click, whatever the model’s PESQ was. This lesson defines RTF, shows why the mean hides the miss, and puts published DeepFilterNet laptop numbers next to an AudioWorklet quantum so they stop being used as phone budgets.

![RTF as wall time over audio time, drawn against a short AudioWorklet callback]({{ site.imgurl }}/generated/rtf-audioworklet.png)

*Figure. RTF $$= T_{\mathrm{wall}}/T_{\mathrm{audio}}$$. The picture’s short block is the render quantum, often 128 samples. At 48 kHz that block lasts $$128/48000\approx 2.67$$ ms. A DeepFilterNet hop is 10 ms of audio and does not have to finish inside one quantum, but the p95 of hop time must stay under 10 ms or the output ring underruns (lesson 05-03).*

## Learning objectives

You will compute RTF from a list of per-frame times, including the exact mean and p95 for the list in the mini-lab; separate that number from algorithmic latency; and set a budget that distinguishes a notebook Core i5, a browser audio thread, and a phone you have not measured.

## 60-minute teaching plan

- **0–10 min** — A mean RTF of 0.43 that still misses the deadline.
- **10–25 min** — Definition, warm-up, percentiles.
- **25–40 min** — 2.67 ms quantum, 10 ms hop, published 0.04 and 0.19.
- **40–50 min** — Cold start versus steady state.
- **50–60 min** — Mini-lab, exercises.

## Core explanation

### Definition

$$
\mathrm{RTF}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{wall}}(\text{process }D\text{ seconds of audio})}{D}.
$$

$$\mathrm{RTF}<1$$ is necessary on **that** machine, **that** build, **that** thread count. It is not sufficient: the operating system can preempt you, a shared core can steal the tail, and the audio device will not wait. For a hop of $$H$$ seconds the audio thread’s budget for everything — STFT, network, copy — is about $$H$$, not “$$H$$ for the net plus free time for the rest.” If the net alone takes $$0.7H$$, 30% remains for the FFT and the ring-buffer copies.

**Algorithmic latency is not RTF.** DeepFilterNet2 and the 2023 demo state **40 ms** of delay for a 20 ms window, a 10 ms hop, and two frames of look-ahead (arXiv:2205.05474, arXiv:2305.08227). That delay is still there when RTF is 0.04. Users wait 40 ms because the filter looked ahead, not because the CPU was slow.

### Percentiles, because the ear hears the tail

Take ten hop times in milliseconds,

$$
[2.0,\;3.0,\;2.5,\;4.0,\;2.2,\;8.0,\;3.1,\;2.8,\;3.4,\;12.0],
$$

and a hop of 10 ms. The mean is $$43.0/10=4.3$$ ms, so mean RTF is $$4.3/10=0.43$$. Sorted, the list is $$[2.0, 2.2, 2.5, 2.8, 3.0, 3.1, 3.4, 4.0, 8.0, 12.0]$$. NumPy’s linear percentile at 95 lands at index $$9\times 0.95=8.55$$, which interpolates between 8.0 and 12.0:

$$
8.0+0.55\times(12.0-8.0)=10.2\text{ ms}.
$$

p95 RTF is $$10.2/10=1.02$$. One interpretation a manager likes (0.43, “lots of headroom”) and one the device hears (a hop that does not finish before the next 10 ms of audio is due). Report p50, p95, and the maximum in a sliding window. A mean of 0.35 with a p99 above 1 clicks about once per hundred hops — every second, at a 10 ms hop.

Measure like this:

1. Warm up. Run on the order of 50 hops before you append a sample, so JIT, the ONNX Runtime arena, and the cache are hot. The first hop is often an allocator spike; report it as cold start, not as RTF.
2. Pin threads. One thread for any WASM comparison. If native ONNX Runtime uses intra-op threads, write the number down; those threads steal the core the audio callback needs.
3. Time only `process`. Do not include the disk read of the wav, and do not include `session` creation.
4. Match the chassis. A phone under thermal throttle is not a cold laptop. DeepFilterNet2’s **RTF 0.04** and the earlier row’s **RTF 0.11** are notebook Core i5-8250U numbers at about 0.35 GMAC. The 2023 demo’s tract loop reports **RTF 0.19** on one thread of an i5-8250U. None of those three figures is a phone RTF.

### Browser versus native, in milliseconds

| Target | Deadline you can compute | What “good” means | Trap |
|--------|--------------------------|-------------------|------|
| AudioWorklet render quantum | 128 samples at 48 kHz: $$128/48000=2.\overline{6}$$ ms, about **2.67 ms** | Do almost no work here; gather quanta into a hop | Running the 10 ms DF hop inside one quantum |
| DF-style hop | 480 samples, **10 ms** | p95 wall time $$\ll 10$$ ms on the target | Quoting mean RTF |
| Native 10 ms callback | 10 ms, shared with AEC | p95 RTF under about 0.5 if AEC still has to run | Giving the whole 10 ms to NS |
| Ultra-light DSP | 1–4 ms class | A small GRU / band model (04-05, 04-06) | Loading DF3 weights |

The quantum length is browser-dependent. Measure `inputs[0][0].length` (lesson 05-02). The 128-sample figure is the common case, not a spec guarantee.

### Cold start is a different timer

Weight download, WASM compile, and ONNX Runtime session creation can take seconds. Do them before the call is audible. The first steady-state hop may still allocate. Exclude it from the percentile or report it on its own line. Field logs that help: `frames_total`, `frames_late` (wall time $$> H$$), `rtf_p95` over a 1 s window, device model, and a thermal flag if the platform has one. Alert when `frames_late / frames_total` leaves the noise floor. Do not upload raw audio to make that graph.

## Pitfalls

- Timing `read()` of the wav inside the hop loop.
- Letting a multi-threaded runtime share the audio core, then blaming the model.
- Publishing a GPU RTF for a CPU ship vehicle.
- Ignoring battery-saver clocks and thermal throttling after twenty minutes.
- Writing “latency 40 ms, so RTF is 0.04.” Those numbers came from different measurements and different questions.

## Mini-lab

**Goal.** From the hard-coded hop times above, print mean RTF and p95 RTF. The expected values are exact for NumPy’s linear percentile.

```bash
python3 - << 'PY'
import numpy as np
times_ms = np.array([2.0, 3.0, 2.5, 4.0, 2.2, 8.0, 3.1, 2.8, 3.4, 12.0])
hop_ms = 10.0
mean_ms = float(times_ms.mean())
p95_ms = float(np.percentile(times_ms, 95))  # linear, index (n-1)*0.95
print(f"mean_ms={mean_ms:.1f}")
print(f"p95_ms={p95_ms:.1f}")
print(f"rtf_mean={mean_ms/hop_ms:.2f}")
print(f"rtf_p95={p95_ms/hop_ms:.2f}")
PY
```

**Expected**. `mean_ms=4.3`, `p95_ms=10.2`, `rtf_mean=0.43`, `rtf_p95=1.02`. The mean says the hop is fine. The p95 says it is not.

**Failure modes**. Using a percentile implementation that picks 12.0 or 8.0 without interpolating, then “failing” a correct 10.2. Dividing by the 20 ms window instead of the 10 ms hop (both RTFs halve). Dropping the 12 ms sample as an outlier you cannot hear. Comparing this toy list with DeepFilterNet2’s published 0.04.

## Exercises

1. **Headroom.** Hop 10 ms, p95 process time 6 ms. What are p95 RTF and the remaining fraction of the hop?
2. **Quantum.** How many 128-sample quanta fit in a 480-sample hop at 48 kHz? Why is the answer not an integer a scheduler can rely on?
3. **Which number.** A colleague pastes “RTF 0.04” into a phone test plan. Which paper, which CPU, and what do you ask them to remeasure?
4. **Incident.** Crackles start after 20 minutes on a laptop that idled at RTF 0.3. List four mechanisms that move p95 without changing the ONNX graph.

### Answer hints

1. RTF $$=6/10=0.60$$. Remaining fraction $$=(10-6)/10=0.40$$ (4 ms for FFT, copies, and the OS).
2. $$480/128=3.75$$. Three quanta are 384 samples, four are 512. You gather with a ring buffer; you do not assume four callbacks per hop.
3. DeepFilterNet2, arXiv:2205.05474, notebook Core i5-8250U, the simplified-model row. Remeasure p95 on the phone, one thread, warm, for at least a few thousand hops. The 0.19 figure (arXiv:2305.08227, tract, same CPU family) is a different binary.
4. Thermal clock drop, a second thread (AEC, GC, a log flush), cache eviction after other tabs allocate, and a state path that gets slower when a buffer grows. The graph file did not change; $$T_{\mathrm{wall}}$$ did.
