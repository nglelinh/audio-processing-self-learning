Before you pick spectral subtraction, a Wiener gain, or DeepFilterNet, you need a shared vocabulary for *what* is corrupting speech. This lesson classifies noise and writes the mixture models that classical and neural methods both assume.

## Core explanations

### Additive noise (the default NS assumption)

$$
y[n] = x[n] + n[n]
$$

Here \(x\) is clean speech, \(n\) is unwanted interference, and \(y\) is what the microphone captures (after ADC). Classical NS and most neural speech enhancement (SE) training recipes start here.

**Stationary** noise: statistics change slowly (HVAC hum). Power spectral density \(S_{nn}(e^{j\omega})\) can be estimated from noise-only regions.

**Non-stationary** noise: statistics change within tens of milliseconds (dishes, keyboard, door slam). Estimators that assume long-term stationarity fail; this is why neural SE and carefully designed trackers matter.

**Babble**: sum of other talkers. Spectrally speech-like; masks that wipe babble often wipe target consonants too.

**Music / media**: harmonic structure; can fool voice-activity detectors (VAD).

### Convolutional degradation (reverb)

$$
y[n] = (x * h)[n] + n[n]
$$

where \(h\) is a room impulse response (RIR). Late reverberation smears energy across time and frequency. **Dereverberation** is related to enhancement but is not identical to NS. Shipping an NS model trained only on additive noise into a cathedral will underperform; DNS Challenge tracks sometimes include RIR augmentation — read track statements carefully.

### Signal-to-noise ratio

Global SNR in dB:

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_n x[n]^2}{\sum_n n[n]^2}
$$

**Segmental SNR** averages short-frame SNRs and correlates better with short-term quality, but both are imperfect proxies for listening tests. Neural SE papers (including DeepFilterNet family) typically report SI-SDR / DNSMOS-style metrics rather than raw SNR improvement alone.

**Numerical intuition.** Suppose clean speech has RMS \(0.1\) (linear full-scale) and you want SNR \(= 0\,\mathrm{dB}\). Then noise RMS should also be \(0.1\), because RMS ratios and power ratios are not the same knob: \(0\,\mathrm{dB}\) means equal *power*, and power is the square of RMS. At SNR \(= -5\,\mathrm{dB}\), noise power is \(10^{0.5}\approx 3.16\times\) speech power, so noise RMS is \(\sqrt{3.16}\approx 1.78\times\) speech RMS — already a hard conversational condition.

**Segmental SNR disagrees with one global number.** Take two equal-length frames with speech energy 1 in each. Frame A has noise energy \(0.01\) (SNR \(20\,\mathrm{dB}\)). Frame B has noise energy \(10^{0.5}\approx 3.16\) (SNR \(-5\,\mathrm{dB}\)). The average of the two frame SNRs is \(7.5\,\mathrm{dB}\). The global SNR uses total energies \(2\) and \(0.01+3.16\):

$$
10\log_{10}\frac{2}{3.17}\approx -2.0\,\mathrm{dB}.
$$

A QA sheet that quotes only the global figure calls this file “below 0 dB.” A sheet that quotes the segmental mean calls it “comfortably positive.” Neither number is the listening test. DeepFilterNet-family results therefore emphasize SI-SDR and DNSMOS-style scores, which still must be read next to the mixture condition you actually shipped.

**What a suppression slider does not change.** npm `deepfilternet3-noise-filter` 1.3.0 exposes `setSuppressionLevel(0–100)` on the public processor. That integer scales how hard a trained suppressor pushes; it does not rewrite \(y=x+n\). A −5 dB babble mixture stays babble at every slider position. The product default rate is 48 kHz full-band, so the noise you must name extends toward a 24 kHz Nyquist even when the vowel formants sit much lower.

### Toy generative model for training

DNS Challenge–style pipelines roughly:

1. Sample clean speech utterance \(x\).
2. Sample noise clip \(n\) (and optionally convolve \(x\) with an RIR \(h\)).
3. Scale \(n\) to hit a target SNR; form \(y = x * h + n\) (or \(y = x + n\)).
4. Train a model to predict \(x\), a mask, or filter coefficients from \(y\) (often in STFT / ERB domains).

This is why **data diversity** beats clever losses when the product domain shifts (motorcycle helmet mics ≠ DNS office noise).

### Single-channel vs multi-channel

| Setup | Observation | Typical tools |
|-------|-------------|---------------|
| Single-channel | one mic \(y\) | spectral NS, RNNoise-class, DeepFilterNet-class |
| Multi-channel | vector \(\mathbf{y}\) | beamforming, GSC, IVA, neural multi-channel SE |

Mezon’s public npm-oriented path is framed around **single-channel real-time** enhancement in browser / WebRTC-like settings. Multi-channel methods appear later only as front-end context (Ch. 03).

## Worked example — mixing at a target SNR

Given speech \(x\) and noise \(n\) with energies \(E_x = \sum x^2\), \(E_n = \sum n^2\), scale noise by \(\alpha\) so that SNR is \(S\) dB. Start from the definition and do not skip the square:

$$
10\log_{10}\frac{E_x}{\alpha^2 E_n} = S
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\frac{E_x}{\alpha^2 E_n}=10^{S/10}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{E_x}{E_n}\cdot 10^{-S/10}}.
$$

When \(E_x=E_n\), the ratio under the square root collapses and \(\alpha=10^{-S/20}\). For \(S=5\), \(\alpha=10^{-0.25}\approx 0.562\), and noise *power* is \(10^{-0.5}\approx 0.316\) times speech power. Then \(y = x + \alpha n\) (truncate or resample to equal length first). Always match sample rates before mixing — a silent bug in many student pipelines. The mini-lab checks both the equal-energy shortcut and a seeded draw whose energies are not equal.

## Common pitfalls

1. Calling everything “noise” including echo (AEC territory) and codec artifacts.
2. Reporting huge SNR gains on near-silent files (division by tiny energy).
3. Training only on stationary noise then testing on babble.
4. Forgetting that **gain mismatch** (different RMS) between train and prod mics shifts effective SNR.
5. Expecting single-channel NS to separate two equal-level talkers reliably.

## Mini-lab

**Goal.** Mix a sine “speech” tone with seeded Gaussian noise at 5 dB SNR and print the scale you actually applied.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fs = 16_000
t = np.arange(fs) / fs
x = 0.1 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 220 * t)
noise = rng.normal(size=fs)
ex, en = np.sum(x**2), np.sum(noise**2)
s_db = 5.0
alpha = np.sqrt((ex / en) * 10 ** (-s_db / 10))
measured = 10 * np.log10(np.sum(x**2) / np.sum((alpha * noise) ** 2))
print(f"alpha={alpha:.6f}")
print(f"snr_db={measured:.2f}")
print(f"alpha_equal_energy={10 ** (-s_db / 20):.6f}")
```

**Expected.** `alpha=0.039870`, `snr_db=5.00`, `alpha_equal_energy=0.562341`. The measured SNR is exactly the target because \(\alpha\) was solved from the two energies; the equal-energy line is the shortcut \(10^{-S/20}\), not the \(\alpha\) of this draw.

**Failure modes.** Scaling the noise by \(10^{-S/10}\) and forgetting the square root (that applies a 10 dB shift when you asked for 5). Mixing a 48 kHz tone with 16 kHz noise without resampling. Computing SNR from RMS values without squaring.

## Mini exercises

1. Classify: keyboard clicks, HVAC, TV dialogue under speech, underground reverb — additive vs convolutional vs both.
2. Compute \(\alpha\) if \(E_x=E_n\) and target SNR \(= 5\,\mathrm{dB}\).
3. Why might SI-SDR improve while listeners hate the audio? Give one artifact class (e.g. musical noise).
4. Sketch how a non-stationary burst fools a noise-floor tracker that updates with a long smoother.

### Answer hints

1. Clicks are additive and impulsive; HVAC is additive and slow; TV dialogue is additive babble; subway reverb is convolutional, usually with additive noise on top.
2. Equal energy and \(S=5\) give \(\alpha=10^{-5/20}\approx 0.562\).
3. Musical noise is a classic: isolated bins pumped on and off. SI-SDR can rise while that texture is unpleasant.
4. A long smoother keeps the pre-burst floor, so the burst is treated as speech and passes. After the burst, the floor stays high and eats the next vowel.
