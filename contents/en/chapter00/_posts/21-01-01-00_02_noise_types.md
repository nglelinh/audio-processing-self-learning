Before you pick spectral subtraction, a Wiener gain, or DeepFilterNet, you need a shared vocabulary for *what* is corrupting speech. This lesson classifies noise and writes the mixture models that classical and neural methods both assume.

## Learning objectives

1. Classify additive, convolutional, and non-stationary degradations with engineering examples.
2. Relate SNR (and segmental SNR) to perceived difficulty and to metric sensitivity.
3. Sketch the single-channel generative model used by DNS-style training pipelines.
4. Explain when reverberation should be treated as a separate problem from NS.
5. Contrast single-channel vs multi-channel observations at a product level.

## Core explanations

### Additive noise (the default NS assumption)

$$
y[n] = x[n] + n[n]
$$

Here \(x\) is clean speech, \(n\) is unwanted interference, and \(y\) is what the microphone captures (after ADC). Classical NS and most neural speech enhancement (SE) training recipes start here.

**Stationary** noise: statistics change slowly (HVAC hum). Power spectral density \(S_{nn}(e^{j\omega})\) can be estimated from noise-only regions.

**Non-stationary** noise: statistics change within tens of milliseconds (dishes, keyboard, door slam). Estimators that assume long-term stationarity fail; this is why neural SE and carefully designed trackers matter.

**Babble**: sum of other talkers. Spectrally speech-like; masks that wipe babble often wipe target consonants too.

**Music / media**: harmonic structure; can fool voice-activity detectors (VAD).

### Convolutional degradation (reverb)

$$
y[n] = (x * h)[n] + n[n]
$$

where \(h\) is a room impulse response (RIR). Late reverberation smears energy across time and frequency. **Dereverberation** is related to enhancement but is not identical to NS. Shipping an NS model trained only on additive noise into a cathedral will underperform; DNS Challenge tracks sometimes include RIR augmentation — read track statements carefully.

### Signal-to-noise ratio

Global SNR in dB:

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_n x[n]^2}{\sum_n n[n]^2}
$$

**Segmental SNR** averages short-frame SNRs and correlates better with short-term quality, but both are imperfect proxies for listening tests. Neural SE papers (including DeepFilterNet family) typically report SI-SDR / DNSMOS-style metrics rather than raw SNR improvement alone.

**Numerical intuition.** Suppose clean speech has RMS \(0.1\) (linear full-scale) and you want SNR \(= 0\,\mathrm{dB}\). Then noise RMS should also be \(0.1\). At SNR \(= -5\,\mathrm{dB}\), noise power is about \(3.16\times\) speech power — already a hard conversational condition.

### Toy generative model for training

DNS Challenge–style pipelines roughly:

1. Sample clean speech utterance \(x\).
2. Sample noise clip \(n\) (and optionally convolve \(x\) with an RIR \(h\)).
3. Scale \(n\) to hit a target SNR; form \(y = x * h + n\) (or \(y = x + n\)).
4. Train a model to predict \(x\), a mask, or filter coefficients from \(y\) (often in STFT / ERB domains).

This is why **data diversity** beats clever losses when the product domain shifts (motorcycle helmet mics ≠ DNS office noise).

### Single-channel vs multi-channel

| Setup | Observation | Typical tools |
|-------|-------------|---------------|
| Single-channel | one mic \(y\) | spectral NS, RNNoise-class, DeepFilterNet-class |
| Multi-channel | vector \(\mathbf{y}\) | beamforming, GSC, IVA, neural multi-channel SE |

Mezon’s public npm-oriented path is framed around **single-channel real-time** enhancement in browser / WebRTC-like settings. Multi-channel methods appear later only as front-end context (Ch. 03).

## Worked example — mixing at a target SNR

Given speech \(x\) and noise \(n\) with energies \(E_x = \sum x^2\), \(E_n = \sum n^2\), scale noise by \(\alpha\) so that SNR is \(S\) dB:

$$
10\log_{10}\frac{E_x}{\alpha^2 E_n} = S \quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{E_x}{E_n}\cdot 10^{-S/10}}
$$

Then \(y = x + \alpha n\) (truncate/resample to equal length first). Always match sample rates before mixing — a silent bug in many student pipelines.

## Common pitfalls

1. Calling everything “noise” including echo (AEC territory) and codec artifacts.
2. Reporting huge SNR gains on near-silent files (division by tiny energy).
3. Training only on stationary noise then testing on babble.
4. Forgetting that **gain mismatch** (different RMS) between train and prod mics shifts effective SNR.
5. Expecting single-channel NS to separate two equal-level talkers reliably.

## Mini exercises

1. Classify: keyboard clicks, HVAC, TV dialogue under speech, underground reverb — additive vs convolutional vs both.
2. Compute \(\alpha\) if \(E_x=E_n\) and target SNR \(= 5\,\mathrm{dB}\).
3. Why might SI-SDR improve while listeners hate the audio? Give one artifact class (e.g. musical noise).
4. Sketch how a non-stationary burst fools a noise-floor tracker that updates with a long smoother.
