Production and field recordings rarely have a clean reference. **Non-intrusive** predictors estimate quality from the enhanced signal alone. **DNSMOS** (from the Microsoft Deep Noise Suppression Challenge lineage) is the predictor you will see most often next to DeepFilterNet-style work. This lesson covers when to use it, how to interpret scores, and how to combine it with listening tests.

## 60-minute teaching plan

- 0–10 min: Why intrusive metrics fail in product logs.
- 10–25 min: DNS Challenge evaluation lineage and DNSMOS role.
- 25–40 min: Score interpretation and domain-shift risks.
- 40–50 min: Automation in CI (conceptual).
- 50–60 min: Mini-lab: design a "no reference" eval folder layout.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you can:

- Explain when DNSMOS is preferable (no clean reference).
- Interpret scores cautiously across domains and languages.
- Combine DNSMOS with listening tests for product decisions.
- Sketch CI usage without treating the predictor as ground truth.

## Intrusive ceiling vs product reality

| Setting | Clean reference? | Typical metrics |
|---------|------------------|-----------------|
| Synthetic DNS-style mixtures | Yes | SI-SDR, PESQ, STOI, … |
| Real customer calls | No | DNSMOS / other MOS predictors + listening |
| A/B shipping decision | Sometimes small labeled set | Predictors **plus** humans |

## DNS Challenge lineage (high level)

Microsoft's **Deep Noise Suppression (DNS) Challenge** series popularized:

- Large noisy/clean training corpora and blind test sets
- A standardized **evaluation protocol** mixing objective metrics and human ratings
- **DNSMOS** — a neural MOS estimator trained to track challenge listening labels

Exact metric lists and DNSMOS variants evolved across challenge years (P.808-style listening, DNSMOS, DNSMOS Pro, etc.). For this course: treat DNSMOS as the **named, well-known non-intrusive predictor** associated with DNS Challenge practice — always cite the specific challenge year / model card you actually run.

## What DNSMOS gives you

- A scalar (or small set of scalars) estimating perceptual quality of a clip **without** $$s$$.
- Offline batch scoring suitable for regression tests on fixed wav sets.

What it does **not** give you:

- A calibrated absolute MOS for every language, device, and codec.
- Permission to skip human listening for UX-sensitive changes.
- Realtime in-browser scoring as a substitute for Chapter 07 telemetry.

## Domain-shift risks

DNSMOS was trained on distributions tied to challenge data and listening protocols. Risk factors:

1. **Language / accent** mismatch vs training listeners and speech.
2. **Codec** artifacts (Opus at low bitrate) confused with NS artifacts.
3. **Music / screen share** audio — out of speech-enhancement domain.
4. **Aggressive NS** that predictors may reward for silence but users hate.
5. **Sample rate / bandwidth** mismatches.

**Mitigation:** freeze a **golden set** of real Mezon-like clips (café, keyboard, fan, echo residual) and track DNSMOS **deltas** across versions rather than obsessing over absolute 3.7 vs 3.8 without context.

## Combining with other signals

Recommended product eval stack:

1. Synthetic: SI-SDR / ΔSI-SDR (08-01)
2. Real/no-ref: DNSMOS (+ optional other predictors you document)
3. Humans: AB or MUSHRA-like (08-03)
4. Systems: RTF, init time, fallback rate (Ch 05–07)

Ship when **(2)+(3)** agree and **(4)** stays in budget — not when (1) alone peaks.

## Automation in CI (conceptual)

```text
on PR affecting NS:
  download golden wavs (or git-lfs)
  run enhancement offline (CLI / headless)
  compute DNSMOS batch
  fail if mean drops > ε vs baseline tag
  upload CSV artifact
```

Keep ε and the golden set under change control. Do not download unbounded internet noise in CI.

## Common pitfalls

1. Comparing DNSMOS across different predictor versions without noting the version.
2. Averaging scores over silence-heavy files.
3. Using DNSMOS on stereo / multi-speaker mess without documenting assumptions.
4. Replacing listening tests entirely.
